Charm pricing: why prices end in 9 in the West and 8 in Asia

The left-digit effect, the evidence behind .99 prices, and why retailers in Chinese-speaking markets end prices on 8 instead.

By the 555558 Editorial Team. Updated October 27, 2026. 8 min read.

Walk through a supermarket in Toronto and most shelf tags end in 9. Walk through a department store in Hong Kong or Kuala Lumpur and a striking share end in 8. Both habits are trying to do the same thing, nudge a buyer toward yes, but they pull on different levers. One works on how people read numbers. The other works on what numbers mean.

The left-digit effect

People read prices from left to right and give the first digit outsized weight. A price of 2.99 gets filed mentally as "two-something" even though it is a cent from three. Marketing researchers Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz showed in a 2005 study in the Journal of Consumer Research that this bias only appears when the leftmost digit changes: 2.99 versus 3.00 feels like a real gap, 3.59 versus 3.60 does not. That is why the useful endings are the ones that sit just below a round threshold.

A widely cited field experiment by Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester ran the same women's clothing item in mail-order catalogues at $34, $39 and $44. The $39 version outsold both, including the cheaper $34 one. The 9-ending did more than signal a lower price; it signalled a deal.

What 9 says beyond "cheaper"

  • Discount cue. Shoppers have learned that 9-endings often appear on sale items, so the ending itself reads as value.
  • Calculation cue. A precise number like 47.99 invites the brain to calculate, which suits rational, comparison-heavy purchases.
  • Quality risk. In luxury, fine dining and professional services, a 9-ending can make an offer look discounted. Round numbers such as 200 or 1,500 feel confident and are processed as a feeling rather than a sum.

Why 8 wins in Chinese-speaking markets

In Mandarin the word for eight, bā, sounds close to fā, part of the word for getting rich. In Cantonese the match is closer still. Because of that, 8 carries a prosperity meaning that shoppers notice. Retail price surveys in China, Hong Kong and Malaysia find prices ending in 8 far more often than Western surveys do, and fewer ending in 4, which sounds like the word for death.

Research on Chinese tourists shopping abroad adds a twist: an 8 in a discount (for example "8% off" or a price of 88) lifted purchase intent more when a Western retailer used it, because buyers read it as respect for their culture. When the retailer was itself Chinese, the effect largely disappeared, because it was expected.

Rule of thumb. Selling to a mainly Western audience on value: end on 9. Selling to Chinese-speaking buyers, or for gifting and celebrations: end on 8, 88 or 888, and keep 4 out of the price. Selling prestige: round numbers.

Don't stack every trick

Price endings are a small lever. They matter most at the moment of comparison: on a shelf, in a search result, on a pricing page with three tiers. They matter far less than whether the product is worth the money. Test one change at a time, run it long enough to see a difference, and watch refund and complaint rates as well as conversion. A price that converts and then gets returned has not helped you.
